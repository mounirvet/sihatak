Bad breath is something almost everyone experiences occasionally, but **persistent** bad breath is different — and it's often a signal worth paying attention to. One of the most common causes is something many people don't suspect: gum disease.

## What causes bad breath?

The most common source of bad breath, medically called **halitosis**, is **bacteria in the mouth**. These bacteria break down food particles and dead cells, releasing volatile sulfur compounds — the molecules behind that unpleasant smell. The more bacteria and the more places they accumulate, the stronger the odor.

## The gum disease connection

Here's the link many people miss: **gum disease is a major cause of persistent bad breath.**

When gums become inflamed (gingivitis) and the condition advances (periodontitis), **pockets** form between the gums and teeth. These pockets trap bacteria and debris in places a toothbrush can't reach, and the bacteria there produce a persistent, hard-to-eliminate odor. So if your breath stays bad despite good brushing, your gums may be the reason.

## Other common causes

- **Poor oral hygiene** that leaves plaque and food behind.
- **A coated tongue**, where bacteria collect on the back of the tongue.
- **Dry mouth**, since saliva normally cleans the mouth and controls bacteria.
- **Certain foods** like garlic and onions.
- **Smoking**, which causes its own odor and worsens gum disease.
- Less commonly, sinus, throat, or digestive issues.

## How to address bad breath

Start with thorough oral care:

- Brush twice daily and **floss** to clean between the teeth.
- **Clean your tongue** gently, including the back.
- Stay **hydrated** to keep saliva flowing.
- Limit odor-causing foods and avoid smoking.

But the key point: if bad breath **persists despite good home care**, that's a strong sign the cause is below the surface — often gum disease — and needs a dentist.

## When to see a dentist

See a dentist if bad breath continues despite good brushing, flossing, and tongue cleaning, or if it comes with bleeding gums, swelling, or gum recession. Treating the underlying gum disease is usually what finally resolves the breath problem — and protects your teeth at the same time.
