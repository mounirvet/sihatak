Your child's smile is built over years, and the habits you establish early shape their dental health for life. This guide walks through what matters at each stage — from that first tooth to the teenage years.

## Before the first tooth

Dental care begins in infancy. Even before teeth appear, gently wipe your baby's gums with a clean, damp cloth after feeds. This removes residue and gets your baby used to having their mouth cleaned.

## Babies (first tooth to age 2)

The first tooth usually appears around 6 months. Once it does:

- Brush **twice daily** with a soft baby toothbrush.
- Use only a **tiny smear** of fluoride toothpaste (rice-grain size).
- Never put your baby to bed with a bottle of milk or juice — a leading cause of early childhood decay.
- Schedule the **first dental visit by age one**.

## Toddlers and young children (ages 2–6)

This is the stage for building habits:

| Focus area | What to do |
|---|---|
| Brushing | Twice daily, you supervise/assist; pea-sized fluoride toothpaste from age 3 |
| Diet | Limit sugary snacks and drinks; offer water and milk |
| Dental visits | Every six months |
| Habits | Gently discourage prolonged thumb-sucking and pacifier use |

Children can't brush effectively on their own until around age 7–8, so your supervision matters.

## School-age children (ages 6–12)

Permanent teeth start coming in around age 6. Key points:

- Encourage independent brushing while checking they do it well.
- Introduce **flossing** once teeth touch.
- Ask your dentist about **fissure sealants** for the back teeth — a protective coating that helps prevent decay.
- Maintain regular checkups.

## Teenagers

Teens face new challenges: more independence (and sometimes lapses in routine), braces, and eventually wisdom teeth. Reinforce good habits, watch for decay around braces, and keep up regular dental visits.

## When to see a dentist

Beyond regular six-month checkups, see a dentist if you notice white or brown spots, toothache, gum swelling, or any injury to the teeth. Early dental visits — starting by age one — are the single best way to keep small problems from becoming big ones.
