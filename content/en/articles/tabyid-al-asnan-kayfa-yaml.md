A brighter smile is one of the most requested cosmetic improvements — and teeth whitening is the most common way to achieve it. But how does it actually work, and is it safe? Here's a science-based look.

## How does whitening work?

Over time, teeth pick up **stains** from food, drinks, smoking, and aging. Whitening products contain **bleaching agents** — typically hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide — that penetrate the enamel and break down the molecules that cause discoloration. The result is a lighter, brighter tooth shade.

It's important to understand: whitening lightens **natural tooth enamel only**. It does not change the color of fillings, crowns, or veneers.

## What are the whitening methods?

| Method | How it works | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| In-office (professional) | Strong gel applied by a dentist, sometimes with light | Fastest, most controlled results |
| Take-home kits (from dentist) | Custom trays + professional-grade gel used at home | Effective, dentist-supervised |
| Over-the-counter | Strips, gels, toothpastes with lower concentrations | Milder, slower, variable results |

Whitening toothpastes mainly remove surface stains and don't change the tooth's underlying shade the way bleaching does.

## Is whitening safe?

For most people, yes — when done properly. The main considerations:

- **Sensitivity** is the most common side effect, and it's usually temporary.
- **Enamel is not damaged** by correctly used, approved whitening products.
- **Overuse** of strong products, or unsupervised use, carries the real risk — which is why professional guidance matters.
- People with **gum disease, untreated cavities, or lots of dental work** should consult a dentist first.

## Why results vary

Whitening doesn't work the same for everyone. It's most effective on yellowish stains, less so on brown or grey ones. Deep stains — such as those from certain medications or injury — may not respond well and might need other cosmetic approaches. And because results fade over time, occasional touch-ups help maintain them.

## When to see a dentist

Talk to a dentist before whitening if you have sensitive teeth, gum disease, untreated cavities, or existing dental work — and if whitening causes lasting sensitivity or uneven results. A dentist can recommend the safest, most effective option for your specific teeth.
